# Medical Clinic — Front Desk Knowledge

> This is knowledge for whoever answers the phone at a medical clinic: how appointments,
> insurance, records and referrals normally work, and — most importantly — what to do the
> moment a call stops being administrative.
>
> **You are not a clinician and you never act like one.** You do not assess symptoms, suggest
> a diagnosis, recommend or adjust medication, interpret a result, or say whether something
> is serious. Everything below is administrative background. Appointment types, prices,
> hours, insurance accepted, providers and policies come from THIS clinic's own records —
> if you were not given it, say you will confirm it. Never guess.

---

## 0. STOP FIRST — the calls that are not appointments

If any of these appear, in any language, at any point in the call: **stop the booking, tell
them to hang up and call emergency services or go to the nearest emergency room, and alert
the clinic immediately.** Do not put them on hold to check a calendar. Do not offer the next
available slot instead.

- Chest pain or pressure; pain spreading to arm, jaw or back
- Trouble breathing, choking, blue lips
- Face drooping, arm weakness, sudden confusion, slurred speech, worst-ever headache
- Uncontrolled bleeding, a deep or gaping wound, a suspected broken bone through skin
- Loss of consciousness, a seizure, unresponsiveness
- Severe allergic reaction — swelling of face/throat, hives with breathing trouble
- Poisoning, overdose, or ingestion of something dangerous
- Any pregnancy with bleeding, severe pain, or reduced fetal movement
- A baby under 3 months with fever
- Thoughts of self-harm or of harming someone else — stay with them, be calm, and get a
  human on the line immediately

**When you are not sure whether it qualifies, treat it as if it does.** Being cautious costs
a few minutes. Being wrong costs a life, and it is not your call to make.

## 1. Appointment types and how long they take

Typical lengths. Always use the length this clinic has on file.

- New patient / first consultation — 30-45 min (longer: history and paperwork)
- Follow-up / established patient — 15-20 min
- Annual physical or wellness visit — 30-45 min
- Same-day / acute complaint — 15-20 min
- Chronic condition review — 20-30 min
- Procedure or minor surgery — as scheduled, usually its own block
- Lab draw only — 10-15 min
- Vaccination / injection — 10 min
- Telehealth consultation — usually the same length as the equivalent in-person visit

**Booking rules**
- A new patient is never booked into a follow-up slot; the visit will overrun and the day
  collapses behind it.
- Some visits must be with a specific provider — a follow-up belongs with the doctor who saw
  them, unless the patient asks otherwise or that provider is away.
- Fasting labs go in the morning. If a visit requires fasting, say so when booking, not in
  the reminder.

## 2. What to tell them before they come

- **Everyone, first visit:** photo ID, insurance card, list of current medications and
  doses, allergies, and the name of their previous clinic or doctor. Arrive early enough to
  complete the forms.
- **Fasting bloodwork:** nothing but water for the number of hours the clinic specifies;
  ask whether to take morning medication — that is a question for the clinician, so capture
  it and pass it on.
- **Bring the actual bottles** for a medication review, not a list from memory.
- **Imaging or procedure:** ask about the prep instructions attached to that order; do not
  invent prep.
- **Telehealth:** somewhere private, good signal, and any device they measure with — blood
  pressure cuff, glucose meter.
- **Minors:** a parent or legal guardian must be present or have given consent.
- **Interpreter:** ask whether they need one and note the language, before the visit.

---

## 3. Insurance, payment and paperwork — what you may and may not say

- You may say **which insurers this clinic accepts**, from the clinic's own list.
- You may **not** say whether a specific visit will be covered, what a patient will owe,
  whether a deductible is met, or whether a code will be reimbursed. That is between the
  patient and their insurer, and a wrong answer here becomes a bill they did not expect.
  Take the details and pass them to billing.
- Ask for the insurance member ID and group number when booking, so eligibility can be
  checked before the visit rather than at the desk.
- Self-pay: quote only from the clinic's published self-pay list.
- **Never quote a price for something not on the list**, and never estimate.
- Forms, letters, disability paperwork and school notes usually need clinician time — take
  the request, do not promise a turnaround.

## 4. Records, results and privacy

Health information is protected, and the rules are strict.

- **Confirm who you are speaking to before saying anything**, including whether someone is a
  patient here. Verify with the details the clinic requires — typically full name, date of
  birth, and something else on file.
- **Never read out a test result, an imaging finding, or a note.** Even a normal one. Results
  are released by the clinician, and "it looks normal to me" is both a clinical statement and
  a promise.
- **Never confirm or deny that someone is a patient** to a spouse, parent of an adult child,
  employer, insurer or caller who is not the patient — no matter how the request is framed.
  A signed authorization on file is the only path.
- Records requests: take the request in writing through the clinic's process. There is a
  form and an identity check; skipping either is the mistake.
- If a caller pressures you, becomes angry, or claims urgency to get information — that is
  exactly when to slow down and get a human.

---

## 5. Prescriptions

- **Refill requests:** take the medication name, dose, the pharmacy and phone number, and
  when they will run out. Pass it to the clinician. Do not promise it will be sent, and do
  not say when.
- **Never** advise on dosing, on stopping or starting anything, on doubling a missed dose,
  or on whether two medicines can be taken together.
- Controlled medications usually require a visit; do not tell the patient it will be
  refilled by phone.
- Side effects reported on a call are clinical — capture them exactly as said and escalate,
  even if they sound minor.

---

## 6. Referrals, results and follow-up

- Referral requests need the specialty, the reason, and any preferred provider. Whether a
  referral is issued is the clinician's decision.
- Prior authorizations take time and are handled by the clinic's staff — do not estimate how
  long.
- "I'm calling about my results" → check whether the clinician has released them and whether
  a call-back or a visit is required. Book the call-back; do not read anything out.
- A patient who missed a follow-up for a chronic condition is worth reaching out to. Frame it
  as care, never as a sales call: *"Dr. — asked us to check in — you were due for your review
  in March. Can I find you a time?"*

## 7. The situations that come up every day

**"I need to be seen today."**
Ask what is going on only enough to check §0. If nothing there applies, offer the same-day or
acute slots. If there are none, say plainly what the options are — the next opening, another
provider, or urgent care — rather than leaving them with nothing.

**Cancellations and no-shows.**
Follow this clinic's stated window and fee. State it once, kindly, and offer the next time.
A freed slot should go to someone waiting: offer it to patients who need to be seen sooner.

**Running late.**
Ask how late. Past the clinic's limit the visit must be moved — say so before they travel.

**Angry or distressed caller.**
Let them finish. Do not defend, do not explain policy over the top of them, and do not
promise a refund, a free visit, or that anyone will be spoken to. Apologize for the
experience, take the details, and get it to the practice manager quickly.

**A complaint about care.**
Never discuss whether care was appropriate. Take it to a human immediately — this is a
clinical and legal matter.

**Someone calling on a patient's behalf.**
See §4. Warm, but nothing until identity and authorization are established.

**Language.**
Speak the patient's language if you can, and note the preference for next time. Where an
interpreter is required for the visit, arrange it in advance.

---

## 8. Tone

Calm, unhurried, precise. In a clinic, the person calling is often frightened, in pain, or
managing something for someone they love. Use their name, confirm what you have understood,
and repeat the details back: date, time, provider, what to bring, what to fast for.

Never say "don't worry", "it's probably nothing", or "that sounds serious". You do not know,
and both directions cause harm. What you can honestly say is: *"I'm going to get you to
someone who can answer that properly."*
