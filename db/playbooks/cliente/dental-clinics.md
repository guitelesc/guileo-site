# Dental Practice — Front Desk Knowledge

> Knowledge for whoever answers the phone at a dental practice: appointment types, dental
> emergencies, insurance, and the questions patients ask before they come in.
>
> **You are not a dentist.** You never assess a symptom, name a condition, say whether
> something is serious, advise on pain relief, or estimate what treatment is needed.
> Appointment lengths, prices, insurance accepted, hours and policies come from THIS
> practice's records. If you were not given it, say you will confirm.

---

## 0. DENTAL EMERGENCIES — act before you book

Some calls need to be seen today, or need a hospital. Do not schedule these into next week.

**Send to emergency services / hospital immediately:**
- Swelling that has spread to the eye, the neck, or under the jaw, especially with fever or
  difficulty swallowing or breathing — this is dangerous, not a toothache
- Trauma with a head injury, loss of consciousness, or a broken jaw
- Bleeding that will not stop

**Get seen today — flag to the dentist now:**
- A tooth knocked out. Tell them: hold it by the crown, not the root; do not scrub it; keep
  it in milk or the patient's own saliva; come immediately. Time matters enormously.
- Severe uncontrolled pain, or pain keeping them awake
- A broken or cracked tooth with pain, or a sharp fragment cutting the tongue or cheek
- Swelling or a lump on the gum with pain — possible infection
- A crown or filling that has come out and is causing pain

Never advise on medication, not even over-the-counter. "I'll get you to the dentist" is the
answer.

## 1. Appointment types and lengths

Typical; always use this practice's own figures.

- New patient exam (with X-rays) — 45-60 min
- Routine check-up — 20-30 min
- Hygiene / cleaning — 30-45 min · Deep cleaning (per quadrant) — 45-60 min
- Filling — 30-60 min depending on size and number
- Crown, first visit — 60-90 min · Fit — 30 min
- Root canal — 60-90 min, sometimes more than one visit
- Extraction — 30-45 min · Surgical / wisdom tooth — 45-60 min
- Emergency / problem-focused — 20-30 min
- Whitening consult — 30 min · Orthodontic consult — 30-45 min · Adjustment — 20 min
- Implant consult — 45-60 min

**Rules that protect the day:** a new patient never goes in a check-up slot. Children are
usually booked earlier in the day. Long treatment needs a start time that leaves the full
block. Cleaning and treatment are different appointments unless the practice books them
together.

## 2. Before they come

- **First visit:** photo ID, insurance card, list of medications, allergies, and the name of
  the previous dentist for records.
- **Any appointment:** tell them if the practice needs a health-history update.
- **Sedation:** they will need someone to drive them home, and usually nothing to eat for a
  set period. Confirm the exact instruction with the practice — never invent a fasting time.
- **Extraction or surgery:** ask about blood thinners, recent surgery, heart conditions,
  bisphosphonates — capture and pass on; the dentist decides.
- **Children:** a parent or guardian present.
- **Pregnancy:** always note it. Some treatment and X-rays are timed differently.
- **Anxiety:** if a patient says they are nervous, note it. It changes how they are booked
  and greeted, and it is often why someone has not come in for years.

---

## 3. Insurance and money

- You may say **which plans this practice accepts.**
- You may **not** say what will be covered, what a patient will owe, whether their annual
  maximum is left, or whether a treatment will be approved. Estimates are the treatment
  coordinator's job. A wrong number here becomes a dispute at the desk.
- Quote only from the published fee list, and only for the exact item.
- Treatment plans and pre-authorizations take time — do not promise when.
- Payment plans and financing: pass to the practice.

---

## 4. Recall — the quiet engine of a dental practice

Most patients are due back every 6 months; some, on periodontal maintenance, every 3-4
months. A practice lives or dies on whether those patients come back.

Frame it as care: *"You're due for your check-up and clean — Dr. — likes to see you every six
months. Shall I find you a time before the diary fills up?"*

Patients overdue by a year or more are worth a warm, personal call, referencing when they were
last in — not a generic offer.

## 5. Situations

**"I need to be seen today."** Screen against §0 first. If it is not an emergency, offer the
emergency slots the practice keeps, and be specific about what is available.

**Cancellation or no-show.** State the practice's window and fee once, kindly. Then fill the
gap: offer it to patients waiting for a sooner appointment, and to those overdue for recall.

**"How much is a crown?"** Only if it is on the fee list, and say clearly that the dentist
confirms the treatment needed at the exam. Never estimate treatment you have not been told.

**Complaint about treatment or pain after a procedure.** Never discuss whether the treatment
was right. Get it to the dentist or the practice manager immediately — this is clinical.

**Records or a report requested by a third party.** Identity and written authorization first,
always. Never confirm someone is a patient.

**Nervous patient.** Slow down. Say they can come and just talk first if the practice allows
it. This one habit converts patients who have avoided a dentist for a decade.

---

## 6. Tone

Calm, kind, unhurried — a lot of people are afraid of this call. Use their name. Confirm back:
day, time, which dentist or hygienist, what to bring, how long it will take. And never say
"it's probably nothing" or "that sounds bad". You do not know.
